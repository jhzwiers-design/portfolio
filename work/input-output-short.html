The brief

A camera reads the silhouette of each person in front of the wall, and the type responds to their body. The silhouette is the input. The typography is the output.

After seeing my work in the Human-Centered Design class, the Chair of the Department of Design invited me to build an interactive piece for input + output, the exhibition of Loyola alum Mitch Paone ’04 and his studio DIA, in the Tools and Legacy section of the Diboll Design Center. DIA’s kinetic type tools and Paone’s rule-based approach to typography were my starting point.

Process

How it came together.

The process in eight steps, one image from each. Hover or tap an image for the note.

8 images · drag or use the arrows

Could I make something like Rakuten Design’s Unlimited Space, where words arrange themselves into the shape of the person in front of the screen?

I sketched eight ways a body could move type in Illustrator, each with its own rule, so I could see which ones felt right before building anything.

From the eight, I kept movement trails, echoes and split canvas, and folded the best of the other five into them.

The main note from feedback was to get a real camera working first.

Each piece got its own words, set in MNKY Wilson, the show typeface.

A second screen so visitors can choose what plays on each wall.

People at the panel have their backs to the walls, so the panel draws the room in isometric view with a live copy of each wall.

The touch screen’s built-in camera watches for motion.

How it works

One computer, two walls, one panel.

One computer between the walls runs the camera and the projectors. A separate touch panel lets visitors change what they see.

CameraUp to three peopleBody tracking finds each person between the walls and draws a silhouette.
The wallsThree piecesInverted Tempo, Echo Field or Emit turns the silhouettes into type on a 9 × 7 ft wall.
Touch panelVisitors chooseThe piece, style, colors and words. A six-letter code links it to the main computer.
PrivacyNever shown or savedThe camera image is never shown or saved. The walls only show silhouettes made of type.
Reflection

Let people play.

The hardest part was working with the older technology in the department and installing the piece myself. I got through it by testing, refining and pushing new iterations until it ran well on the computers we had.

My favorite piece is Echo Field. When several people play together, their echoes ripple into each other, and one person’s silhouette meets another person’s echo.

“I was inspired by exhibits at children’s museums, where the interactions feel almost effortless. I hope visitors make that connection and let themselves play.”
